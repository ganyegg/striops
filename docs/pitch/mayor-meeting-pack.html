02The sixty-second answer

If the Mayor asks “what exactly is this?”
Striops is a Strategic Intelligence Operating System. It keeps a continuously-updated, source-linked model of the city — money, water, energy, safety, waste — and runs forecasting, risk and simulation over it to produce a daily executive brief with recommended decisions. Every number carries its source. The AI writes the prose; it never writes the arithmetic. It is not a dashboard, not a consultancy report, and not a chatbot on a data lake.
If the Mayor asks “why would I buy this?”
Because your quarterly reports are true on the day they are printed and stale by the time you act on them. Striops holds the same facts, refreshed continuously, and it remembers why every decision was made. Non-revenue water alone costs the City roughly R185 million a year for every percentage point of system input lost. The pilot costs R180,000. If it changes one decision by a thousandth of a percentage point, it has paid for itself.

Both answers are drawn from docs/STRIOPS.md §0 and §7 and are consistent with the pitch deck.

Crime is now as current as SAPS allows

The extract runs to March 2026 — the last quarter SAPS has released. It is read directly from the station-level sheet inside each SAPS quarterly workbook and summed across Cape Town's 62 precincts, so the remaining lag is the national publication cycle and nothing else.

If asked: “SAPS publishes a quarter at a time, so March is the newest official month in the country. Striops reads their station sheet directly and labels the lag rather than hiding it. Wire the City's own precinct extract in the pilot and it becomes monthly.”

One caveat before you quote the crime movement

Pulse reads “murders up 18.6%, February to March 2026” (231 to 274). That is the published monthly count, but February is a 28-day month — per day the rise is nearer 7%.

Quote the levels, not the month-on-month percentage. Over the quarter the direction is genuinely down: 781 murders in January–March 2026 against 864 in the same quarter of 2025.

05The artefacts, and how the system handles them

Two City service series broke range in June 2026. The Pulse used to assert them as month-on-month findings, which would not have survived contact with the relevant director. The system now screens them and says so. The demo is stronger because of this, not weaker — lead with it rather than hoping nobody clicks.

How the screen works

A reading more than twice — or less than half — the median of its own previous six periods is marked awaiting verification instead of being reported as a change. Those rows are dropped from the worsening and improving counts and sorted below the metrics that can defend themselves. The rule is deliberately blunt so you can restate it in one sentence in the room.

SeriesJune 2026Median, prior 6BreakNow shown as
Refuse service requests28,60411,5292.5× Awaiting verification
Public lighting outages43,5889,3574.7× Awaiting verification
Both break in the same month, which points at one shared cause — most likely a change in how C3 service requests are categorised or de-duplicated. The refuse extract also records 336 for September 2025, which is plainly broken and is screened on the same rule.
And July proves the point

July 2026 came back to baseline — 10,205 refuse requests and 9,719 lighting faults, both squarely in the historical range. So June was a transient extract fault, not a service collapse. The rows stay flagged because June is the comparison month, and any June-to-July percentage would be just as false in the opposite direction.

Say this when the flag appears on screen
That row is the product refusing to lie to you. Refuse complaints did not treble in June — the extract changed, and July came back to normal. A quarterly report would have printed the spike as a service collapse and someone would have spent a month responding to it.

Still do not present these two as findings

Two different dam levels, both live. The City series shows 61.35% for January 2026 and stops there; the DWS system reading is 78.2% for the week of 10 August 2026. They are seventeen points apart because they measure different things over different periods. Present the DWS figure as the current position and the City series as the City's own historical record — never side by side without that sentence. The comparison worth making is year-on-year: 78.2% now against 88.1% the same week last year, roughly ten percentage points down.

The underspend amounts. The R1.21bn on Water and Sanitation and R0.88bn on Roads and Transport come from demonstration full-year function totals, not the City's official mid-year position for FY2025/26. The system labels them and so must you. Sell the method, not the amount: “this is the shape of the analysis — wire the S52 tables in the pilot and it becomes your number.”

06Before you walk in

The engineering blockers are cleared. What is left is sequencing and rehearsal.

  1. Brief the officials before you request the meeting. The City's published process asks you to list who you have already consulted — so this is not optional courtesy, it is a requirement of the request. Start with Gareth Morgan (ED: Future Planning and Resilience) and Kevin Jacoby (CFO). Brief Jason Mc Neil (Urban Waste Management) too, so the refuse artefact reaches him from you rather than from the Mayor.
  2. Rehearse the two flagged rows out loud. You should be the one who introduces them. The script is in section 05 and it takes fifteen seconds.
  3. Clear the two overdue actions or restate their dates. act-refuse-capacity was due 30 June and act-pipe-programme-report 31 July. An accountability product showing its own overdue items is either an honesty asset or an unforced error, depending entirely on whether you raise it first.
  4. Open the live site once, the morning of. It is current and all routes serve, but a cold free-tier instance takes about sixty seconds to wake. Load it before you walk in so the first click in front of the Mayor is instant.
Turn the overdue actions into the argument

“Two of the five actions in here are overdue, and the system says so on its own front page. That is the point. A report cannot embarrass you in nine months' time. This can, which is why it changes behaviour.”

Fixed this week

  • Production is current. Three weeks of work had never reached GitHub, so Render had nothing to deploy. All routes now serve today's build, verified end to end.
  • Crime is current to March 2026. The previous extract came from a volunteer project that stopped updating in December 2025, freezing Cape Town at September 2025. Striops now reads the station-level sheet inside each SAPS quarterly workbook directly. The new parser reproduces the old extract exactly across all 69 overlapping months, and adds six.
  • Outlier screening. Refuse and lighting are flagged rather than reported as findings, and excluded from the headline counts.
  • The Wins page no longer contradicts the Pulse. It claimed streetlight faults were falling 2.7% per period, from a stale seed series, while the Pulse read up 116%. Curated wins that cite a metric are now re-tested against that live series on every read; anything unsupported renders as Under review.
  • No more future-dated numbers. The City's energy dataset carries rows for the rest of the financial year alongside actuals, so the site was reporting “system energy down 9.5%, November to December 2026” — in August. Points beyond the current month are now dropped at ingest and again when the Pulse is built.
  • Dam storage is current and comparative. The DWS weekly reading reaches the Pulse and carries the published year-ago figure, so the movement means something.

08The ask

Do not ask for a budget decision in the room. Ask for the two things that make the next conversation inevitable.

One

A data-sharing MOU, aggregates only

No citizen personal information in scope. The two extracts that unlock the most value: Finance S52 year-to-date operating and capital tables, and Water and Sanitation's official annual system input and zone-level non-revenue water. Those turn the largest demonstration numbers in this pack into official ones.

Two

A named sponsor and a 90-day pilot

R180,000, measured against a written success test agreed up front. A named executive sponsor matters more than the money — without one, the feeds never arrive.

The close
Give me two feeds and ninety days. If at the end of it the daily brief has not changed a single decision, do not renew — and you keep the data, the exports and the schema regardless. There is no proprietary lock on your own facts.

How the City requires the meeting to be requested

All meetings are scheduled at the Executive Mayor's discretion, and the published process asks for four things in writing:

  1. A clear statement of your reason for wanting to meet.
  2. A list of all officials you have consulted before requesting the meeting.
  3. A proposed agenda.
  4. A list of proposed attendees and their designations.

Submit by email to mayor.mayor@capetown.gov.za, or hand-deliver into the Mayor's internal post box at the Podium Block information desk, Civic Centre.

Act on point 2 first. A request that lists Gareth Morgan and Kevin Jacoby as already consulted is materially stronger than a cold one, and it is the cheapest thing in this pack to fix.
